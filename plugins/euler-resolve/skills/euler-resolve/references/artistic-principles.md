# Artistic Principles

Structure (math) and perception (biology) tell you what will read correctly. This is what makes it feel intentional instead of mechanical.

## Elements vs. principles

The **elements** are the raw material: line, shape, form, space, texture, value, color. The **principles** are what you do with them: balance, contrast, emphasis, movement, pattern/rhythm, proportion, unity. A composition can use all the right elements and still fail if the principles governing their arrangement are absent — this is the usual gap between "has all the right pieces" and "actually works."

## Balance

- **Symmetrical** — mirrored weight across an axis. Stable, formal, can tip into static if overused (see anti-patterns).
- **Asymmetrical** — unequal *arrangement* but equal *visual weight* (see the weight heuristics in perception-and-biology.md — a small dark shape can balance a large pale field). Harder to get right than symmetry, reads as more dynamic and more considered when it works.
- **Radial** — weight balanced around a center point rather than an axis.

## Emphasis and focal point

A focal point is created relatively, not absolutely — by making one element different from its surroundings on at least one strong channel:
- **Contrast** — in value, color, scale, or texture against the surrounding field.
- **Isolation** — surrounded by negative space while everything else is grouped, or vice versa.
- **Convergence** — leading lines, implied gaze direction, or perspective lines that terminate at it (uses Gestalt continuity).
- **Anomaly** — the one element that breaks an established pattern (only works if the pattern is established clearly enough first for the break to register).

A composition needs exactly one primary focal point. Secondary and tertiary points of interest are fine and often necessary, but they need to be visibly subordinate (weaker on whatever channel the primary point uses) or they compete and the hierarchy collapses.

## Movement and rhythm

Implied motion comes from repetition with a directional trend: gradation (elements that change size/spacing/value progressively across the frame), radiating lines, or a directional gesture in the linework itself. Rhythm types, roughly in order of how much energy they read as having:
- **Regular** — identical intervals. Calm, can read as static or mechanical.
- **Alternating** — a repeating A-B-A-B pattern. More energy than regular, still predictable.
- **Progressive/gradation** — intervals that change steadily (spacing tightening, size growing). Strong sense of direction/movement.
- **Flowing** — organic, irregular-but-connected rhythm, closest to natural/hand-drawn variance.

## Proportion and scale

Beyond the mathematical ratios in mathematical-foundations.md, proportion also means relationship to a *known* reference — most often the human body/hand/eye, since that's the scale humans intuitively judge everything against. Deliberately violating expected proportion (oversized/undersized relative to that reference) is one of the most reliable ways to create surreal, monumental, or unsettling effect — it only works, though, if the "correct" proportion was legible enough first for the violation to register as a choice.

## Unity and variety

These are in tension on purpose. Pure unity (everything repeated identically) reads as monotonous; pure variety (nothing repeated) reads as chaotic/unresolved. Working compositions establish a repeated element or system (a shape language, a palette, a rhythm) — that's the unity — and then vary it within that system rather than either freezing it or abandoning it. This is the same idea as the fractal/natural-variance note in mathematical-foundations.md: the system is consistent, the instances within it aren't identical.

## Negative space

Negative space is not empty/leftover area — it's an active shape with its own weight, and it's often what makes a focal point read as intentional rather than crowded. Two failure directions, both covered in anti-patterns.md: too little (horror vacui, everything crowds the edges, nothing has room to be the focal point) and too much without purpose (isolated elements floating with no compositional reason for the emptiness around them).

## Hierarchy encoding

Concretely, "hierarchy" (which element is read first, second, third) is encoded through some combination of: size, value/contrast, color saturation, position (power points, entry points from the scan patterns in perception-and-biology.md), and isolation. The specific channel matters less than *consistency* — if size means importance in one part of a composition, a same-size-but-more-important element elsewhere will read as equally important, not more, because the viewer has already learned the code being used.
